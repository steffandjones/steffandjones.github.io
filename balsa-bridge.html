Balsa Wood Bridge

ES120: Structural Mechanics · Civil Engineering

Project Overview

ES120: Engineering Sciences 120 (Structural Mechanics) is Harvard's civil engineering fundamentals course. The final project challenged students to design and build a lightweight balsa wood bridge capable of supporting maximum load relative to its own weight. Our bridge lifted approximately 250lbs — nearly 20 times its own weight. The bridge failed at the glue joints; Professor Vlassak noted that had the adhesive held, it would have been capable of lifting significantly more.

Key Achievements

  • Lifted ~250lbs, nearly 20:1 load-to-weight ratio
  • Failed at glue joints; structural capacity estimated well beyond recorded load
  • Optimized truss geometry through FEA simulations
  • Minimized material usage while maintaining strength
  • Precise fabrication with tolerances under 1mm
  • Successful load testing to failure point

Technical Details

Structural Design: Employed Warren truss geometry — a triangulated arrangement of diagonal members — selected because it distributes load so that each member carries primarily axial force (tension or compression) rather than bending, making efficient use of balsa wood's high strength along the grain. Members were oriented so grain direction aligned with the primary load axis in each element. Reinforcements were added only at the joints predicted by FEA to see the highest stress concentrations, avoiding unnecessary weight additions elsewhere.

Analysis: Finite element analysis in ANSYS was used to model the full truss under the applied loading, generating stress and deflection distributions across every member. This allowed the design to be iterated computationally before fabrication — cross-sections were adjusted, members added or removed, and reinforcement positions refined based on where the model showed stress concentrations. The model identified the glue joints at the most heavily loaded nodes as the critical failure point, a prediction that was confirmed when the bridge failed there during load testing.

Fabrication: Construction required methodical assembly: each member was cut to length, grain direction verified, and dry-fitted before any adhesive was applied. Glue quantities were carefully controlled — too little produced a weak joint, too much added weight without a proportional strength gain. Dimensional tolerances were held under 1mm throughout. When the bridge failed under test, it failed at the adhesive joints rather than within the balsa members. Professor Vlassak's assessment was that the structural design had significant headroom beyond the recorded failure load, and that with stronger joint execution the bridge would have carried considerably more.
